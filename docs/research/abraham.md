## 2. Islamic tradition

### His father

Ibn Kathir (on 6:74) reports that Ibn Abbas said the father's name was Tarih, not Azar («إن أبا إبراهيم لم يكن اسمه آزر ، إنما كان اسمه تارح»); Mujahid and al-Suddi said Azar is the name of an idol («آزر : اسم صنم»); Ibn Jarir held that Azar is his name and that he may have had two. Ibn Kathir's own remark: «كأنه غلب عليه آزر لخدمته ذلك الصنم».

### Where he was born

- **Babylon, the land of the Chaldeans**, which Ibn Kathir calls «الصحيح المشهور عند أهل السير والتواريخ والأخبار».
- **Barza, in the Ghouta of Damascus**, by Mount Qasiyun: Ibn 'Asakir from Ibn Abbas, through Makhul («ولد إبراهيم بغوطة دمشق، في قرية يقال لها: برزة، في جبل يقال له: قاسيون»). Ibn 'Asakir himself concludes «والصحيح أنه ولد ببابل» and explains the Barza site as where Abraham prayed when he went to help Lot.
- **Al-Sus, Babylon, or al-Sawad near Kutha**: «وكان مولده بالسوس، وقيل: ببابل. وقيل: بالسواد من ناحية كوثى».
- **Not Harran**: since Terah died in Harran, «هذا يدل على أنه لم يولد بحران».

### The king who argued with him (2:258)

- **Nimrod son of Kan'an son of Kush son of Sam son of Noah, king of Babylon**, in Mujahid's view: «وهذا الملك هو ملك بابل، واسمه النمرود بن كنعان بن كوش بن سام بن نوح». Another lineage makes him Nimrod son of Falikh son of 'Abir.
- **He reigned 400 years**, it is said: «وذكروا أن نمرود هذا استمر في ملكه أربعمائة سنة».
- **One of four who ruled the whole earth** (Mujahid): two believers, Solomon and Dhu al-Qarnayn, and two disbelievers, Nimrod and Nebuchadnezzar (Bukhtnassar). Al-Tabari, narration 5874: «فالمؤمنان: سليمان بن داود وذو القرنين، والكافران: بختنصر ونمرود بن كنعان».
- **Mosul, not Babylon**, in one narration in al-Tabari (Ibn Zayd, 5876): «هو نمروذ كان بالموصل والناس يأتونه».
- **After the fire**: al-Suddi says the debate took place after Abraham came out of the fire (Ibn Kathir; al-Tabari 5878: «لما خرج إبراهيم من النار، أدخلوه على الملك»).
- **His end**: Zayd ibn Aslam's report of a gnat that entered his nose and stayed 400 years (Ibn Kathir; al-Tabari 5875).
- **Ibn Jarir's history** places Abraham's birth in Nimrod's time, and "it is said" that this king is **al-Dahhak**: «ذكر ابن جرير في تاريخه، أن مولده كان في زمن النمرود بن كنعان» … «وهو فيما قيل الضحاك الملك المشهور، الذي يقال: إنه ملك ألف سنة» (Ibn Kathir, «وفاة إبراهيم»).

### The stars, and Harran

Ibn Kathir holds that the sermon on the star, the moon and the sun was addressed to the people of **Harran**, who worshipped the planets and idols, while the idols he broke were those of **Babylon**: «والظاهر أن موعظته هذه في الكواكب لأهل حران، فإنهم كان يعبدونها»; «وأما أهل بابل فكانوا يعبدون الأصنام». On 6:79 he insists that Abraham was **debating** his people, not searching for God himself: «والحق أن إبراهيم عليه الصلاة والسلام ، كان في هذا المقام مناظرا لقومه». The planets meant are the seven «الكواكب السيارة السبعة المتحيرة». He rejects as Israelite lore Ibn Ishaq's story that Abraham said these words as a child leaving the cave where he was hidden from Nimrod.

### The fire

Ibn Kathir reports that they put Abraham in the cup of a catapult made for them by a Kurd called Hazan («في كفة منجنيق صنعه لهم رجل من الأكراد يقال له هزن»), and, from al-Minhal, that he stayed in the fire forty or fifty days. No age and no city are given.

### The emigration

Abraham left with Sarah (his cousin, the daughter of Haran, in the best-known view) and his nephew Lot. Terah died in Harran aged 250, following the People of the Book: «فنزلوا حران فمات فيها تارخ، وله مائتان وخمسون سنة». Ka'b al-Ahbar held that the blessed land of 21:71 is Harran; the majority (Ubayy, Abu al-'Aliya, Qatada) say al-Sham, which Ibn Kathir follows. From al-Sham they went down to Egypt, and returned to the land of Bayt al-Maqdis; Lot settled at Sodom.

### The king of Egypt and Sarah

The hadith in al-Bukhari and Muslim calls him only a tyrant (جبار). Ibn Kathir then gives three later claims, each marked as hearsay:

- «وذكر بعض أهل التواريخ، أن فرعون مصر هذا كان أخًا للضحاك الملك المشهور بالظلم، وكان عاملًا لأخيه على مصر» (he was al-Dahhak's brother and governor of Egypt);
- «ويقال: كان اسمه سنان بن علوان، بن عبيد، بن عويج، بن عملاق، بن لاود، بن سام، بن نوح»;
- from Ibn Hisham's *al-Tijan*, through al-Suhayli: «أن الذي أرادها عمرو بن امرىء القيس، بن مايلون، بن سبأ، وكان على مصر» — followed by «فالله أعلم».

Hagar was given to Sarah by this king: «وصحبتهم هاجر القبطية المصرية».

### Hagar, Ishmael and Jurhum

From al-Bukhari's long hadith of Ibn Abbas (through Sa'id ibn Jubayr), as Ibn Kathir quotes it: Mecca had no one and no water («وليس بمكة يومئذ أحد، وليس بها ماء»); then «مرت بهم رفقة من جرهم، أو أهل بيت من جرهم», who settled in lower Mecca; Ishmael grew up among them, learned Arabic from them, and married among them. The page names no Jurhum leader and neither of Ishmael's wives.

### Ages and death

Abraham was 86 at Ishmael's birth (the People of the Book), was circumcised at 120 and lived 80 years more (a hadith of Abu Hurayra), and died at 175 or 195 (the People of the Book) or 200 (Ibn al-Kalbi). He was buried in the cave at Hebron, «وهو البلد المعروف بالخليل اليوم»; no sound report fixes the exact spot.

## 3. Other traditions

### The Hebrew Bible (Genesis 11:26–25:10)

- Terah was 70 when Abram was born (11:26). Haran died "in Ur of the Chaldees" (11:28). The family left Ur for Canaan but settled in Haran, where Terah died at 205 (11:31–32). Abram left Haran at 75 (12:4).
- A Pharaoh, unnamed, takes Sarai (12:10–20).
- **The war of the kings** (14:1): "In the days of Amraphel, king of Shinar; Arioch, king of Ellasar; Chedorlaomer, king of Elam; and Tidal, king of Goiim". Melchizedek, king of Salem, blesses Abram (Josephus adds that Salem was later called Jerusalem, *Ant.* 1.10.2).
- Abram was 86 at Ishmael's birth (16:16) and 100 at Isaac's (21:5), and lived 175 years (25:7).

### Jewish writings older than Islam

- **The Book of Jubilees** (2nd century BCE; the *Jewish Encyclopedia* dates it "probably in the time of John Hyrcanus"). Abram is born in the year of the world 1876 (11:13); he separates from his father's idols as a youth (11:16); at 60 "Abram arose by night, and burned the house of the idols" (12:12); his brother Haran dies trying to save them, "in Ur of the Chaldees" (12:14); the family settles in Haran; there Abram watches the stars on a new moon and concludes that "all the signs of the stars, and the signs of the moon and of the sun are all in the hand of the Lord" (12:16–17); he then leaves Haran for Canaan (12:28).
- **Pseudo-Philo, *Biblical Antiquities*** (a Jewish book of the first century CE, surviving in Latin, translated from Greek, translated from Hebrew). When Babylon's builders make bricks for the tower, twelve men refuse, Abram first among them. The princes "Phenech and Nemroth" (Nimrod) and the leader Jectan have Abram put into a furnace "with the bricks"; "God stirred up a great earthquake, and the fire gushed forth of the furnace", and Abram comes out unharmed (6:16–18). The editor notes: "Most of the stories make Nimrod the principal actor."
- **The rabbis**, as summarized by the *Jewish Encyclopedia* ("Abraham", 1901–1906): Terah made and sold idols (Genesis Rabbah 38; Tanna debe Eliyahu 2:25); Nimrod "ordered Abraham to be cast into a furnace"; Nimrod is Amraphel (Pesiqta Rabbati 33; Eruvin 53a); and Abraham "was born in Kuta, another name for Ur of the Chaldees" (Bava Batra 91a). **The same Kutha appears in the Islamic report quoted above.** The encyclopedia adds: "The legend betrays Persian influence."

### Greek and Roman writers

- **Berosus**, the Babylonian priest (3rd century BCE), as quoted by Josephus (*Ant.* 1.7.2): "In the tenth generation after the flood, there was among the Chaldeans a man, righteous, and great, and skilful in the celestial science". Berosus names no one; Josephus takes it to mean Abram. The *Jewish Encyclopedia* warns that Alexandrian Jews of the 3rd–2nd centuries BCE wrote "under the name of Hecatæus and Berosus", so these quotations may not be genuine.
- **Hecataeus** "composed, and left behind him, a Book concerning him" (Josephus, *Ant.* 1.7.2), with the same caution.
- **Nicolaus of Damascus** (1st century BCE), *History* book 4, quoted by Josephus (*Ant.* 1.7.2): "Abram reigned at Damascus; being a foreigner, who came with an army out of the land above Babylon", called the land of the Chaldeans; "there is shewed a village named from him, The Habitation of Abram".
- **Pompeius Trogus** (1st century BCE), through Justin's *Epitome* 36.2: "the origin of the Jews was from Damascus"; "After Damascus, Azel was King, and afterwards Ador, and Abraham, and Israhel."
- **Josephus** himself: Abram taught the Egyptians arithmetic and astronomy (*Ant.* 1.8.2), and the Pharaoh is not named.

Damascus thus appears in three unrelated memories: Nicolaus and Trogus make Abraham a king there, and Ibn 'Asakir has him born at Barza, outside the city.

## 4. What history and archaeology have

- **No inscription names Abraham** as a person.
- **"Fort of Abram" (?):** in the list of places that Sheshonq I (the biblical Shishak) claimed in the Negev, carved at Karnak about 925 BCE, two signs read "p.hqr ʾbrm, 'Fort of Abram' (?)", with the question mark (K. A. Kitchen). J. H. Breasted proposed "Field of Abram" in 1904 (*AJSL* 21). If the reading is right, it shows a place named after an Abram in the Negev in the 10th century BCE; it does not date the patriarch.
- **Camels:** radiocarbon dating at copper-smelting sites in the Aravah places the arrival of domestic camels in the southern Levant in the last third of the 10th century BCE or later (L. Sapir-Hen and E. Ben-Yosef, in *Tel Aviv*; release of 3 February 2014). The release notes that Genesis has camels in the stories of Abraham, Joseph and Jacob, and draws the conclusion that the text was written down well after the events; it gives "the Age of the Patriarchs (2000–1500 BCE)" as the conventional period.
- **Ur:** CDLI dates the Third Dynasty of Ur to "ca. 2100–2000 BC". Its end is told in the Sumerian "Lament for Sumer and Urim": "that Ibbi-Suen should be taken to the land Elam in fetters", and "Urim … is to be counted as a ruin-mound".
- **Writing:** CDLI dates the Uruk IV period to "ca. 3350–3200 BC" and Uruk III to "ca. 3200–3000 BC". The oldest proto-cuneiform tablets belong to Uruk IV (a lead from CDLI's wiki, which refused automated access).
- **Leads not yet read:** the moon god Sin's temple at Harran (Ehulhul) and at Ur (Nanna), and Nabonidus's Harran inscriptions of the 6th century BCE; the Beni Hasan painting of 37 "Aamu" led by Abisha, visiting Egypt under Senwosret II (about 1890 BCE), often shown to illustrate the patriarchs' world; Hammurabi's dates; the scholarly debate on the patriarchs' historicity (W. F. Albright's early-2nd-millennium "patriarchal age" against T. L. Thompson, 1974, and J. Van Seters, 1975).

### Babylon, and who ruled in 2500–2000 BCE

The owner asked when Babylon was built. The pages that would confirm this (UNESCO, Britannica, the Met) refused automated access, so these are **leads from search results and standard chronology, to be verified**:

- **No founding date is known.** Babylon's earliest levels lie below the water table and have hardly been excavated.
- **The earliest known mention** is a year-name of **Shar-kali-sharri**, king of Akkad (about 2217–2193 BCE), recording the building of temples in Babylon.
- Under the **Third Dynasty of Ur** (about 2100–2000 BCE, CDLI) Babylon was a provincial town with a governor.
- It became a royal capital with the **Amorite First Dynasty** (Sumu-abum, about 1894 BCE) and an imperial one under **Hammurabi** (about 1792–1750 BCE).

So in 2500–2000 BCE Babylon was at most a small town, and only from about 2200 BCE. When the traditions call Nimrod "king of Babylon", they may mean the region rather than the city: Ibn Kathir himself writes «أرض الكلدانيين، يعنون أرض بابل».

Rulers of 2500–2000 BCE who could be set beside Abraham (conventional middle-chronology dates; leads except Ur III):

- **Sumer:** city-states (Kish, Ur, Uruk, Lagash).
- **Akkad:** the first empire "of the four quarters", under Sargon (about 2334–2279), Naram-Sin and Shar-kali-sharri.
- **After Akkad:** the Gutians, then Gudea of Lagash.
- **Ur III:** Ur-Nammu, Shulgi, and Ibbi-Sin, who was taken to Elam (CDLI; ETCSL).
- **Egypt:** the late Old Kingdom, then the First Intermediate Period, then the start of the Middle Kingdom.

Genesis 10:10 begins Nimrod's kingdom with "Babel, Erech, Accad, and Calneh, in the land of Shinar". Some scholars have read the biblical Nimrod as a memory of the kings of Akkad (lead: Y. Levin, *Vetus Testamentum* 52, 2002). This would fit the tradition that he was one of the kings who ruled the whole earth. It is an identification proposed by modern scholars, not a statement of the sources.

**Both of the owner's estimates agree with some of the sources.** The first (about 1975–1475) fits the app's current span (2000–1826), the Septuagint reckoning and the conventional 2000–1500. The second (2500–2000) contains the Hebrew Bible's numbers (born about 2166) and agrees with Ibn Kathir's lower bound. If Abraham's main span moved to 2166–1991, the reckoning before him (D20, which places Noah's death a thousand years before Abraham's birth) would move back by about 166 years.

It is also a period of **named rulers**, so the figures around Abraham can be set beside real ones: the last kings of the Third Dynasty of Ur and the fall of Ur to Elam (about 2000 BCE, CDLI and ETCSL); Egypt's Middle Kingdom, the time of the Beni Hasan "Aamu" (lead); and Old Babylonian kings such as Hammurabi (lead). Writing had existed for over a thousand years by then (proto-cuneiform from Uruk IV, about 3350–3200 BCE, CDLI).

## 7. Project observations (speculation, clearly labeled)

- **The end of Ur and the Elamite king.** Ur III's empire ended about 2000 BCE when Ibbi-Sin was taken to Elam. Genesis 14 has an Elamite king, Chedorlaomer, leading the eastern kings. The traditional date of Abraham's leaving (about 2000 BCE) falls at the same horizon.
- **The moon at Ur and Harran.** Ur and Harran, the two cities of Abraham's early life in Genesis, were both later known as cities of the moon god (lead, not yet sourced). The Quran's Abraham turns from the star, the moon and the sun (6:76–78), and Ibn Kathir places that sermon at Harran among star-worshippers.
- **Kutha twice.** The Babylonian Talmud (Bava Batra 91a) and an Islamic report both name Kutha as his birthplace.
- **The fire before Islam.** Jubilees (Abraham burns the idols' house), Pseudo-Philo (Abraham in a furnace for refusing to build the tower) and the rabbis (Nimrod's furnace) all predate Islam; the Quran gives its own account, in which God makes the fire cool and safe (21:69).

## 8. What was built (2026-11-26)

The owner chose the Hebrew Bible's anchor (Abraham born about 2166 BCE, D24) and asked for all of the following, with Islamic sources leading and the People of the Book neither affirmed nor denied (D25). Built as drafts: the enriched `abraham` entry; eight episodes (`abraham-idols-fire`, `abraham-and-the-king`, `abraham-stars-harran`, `abraham-emigration`, `sarah-king-of-egypt` in the Egypt lane, `hagar-ishmael-mecca`, `abraham-sacrifice`, `abraham-guests`); and `ur-iii` and `fall-of-ur` in the Mesopotamia lane. The original proposal follows.

### The proposal

1. **Enrich the Abraham entry:** place opinions (Babylon, Kutha, Barza, Hebron), tradition (Nimrod, the king of Egypt, al-Dahhak, Jurhum), alternatives (the Hebrew Bible 2166–1991 and the Septuagint 1951–1776), history (no inscription, "Fort of Abram", camels) and a draft connection.
2. **New Quran-lane episodes,** each placed inside Abraham's span: the idols and the fire; the argument with the king; the star, the moon and the sun; the emigration; Hagar and Ishmael at Mecca; the sacrifice; the guests. "Abraham and Ishmael raise the foundations of the House" already exists.
3. **A tradition episode** for Sarah and the king of Egypt (not in the Quran; hadith and Genesis).
4. **Context in other lanes:** the Third Dynasty of Ur (Mesopotamia lane, about 2100–2000 BCE) and the fall of Ur to Elam.
5. **Dating:** keep 2000–1826 as the main span, which fits the owner's estimate of 4,000–3,500 years ago, and add the Hebrew Bible and Septuagint reckonings as alternatives.
